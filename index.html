Redesigning the iPhone Phone App for Deaf & Hard-of-Hearing Users

A phone's most fundamental feature is making phone calls. We asked a simple question: does this basic experience actually work for everyone? It doesn't.

Around 48 million Americans have some degree of hearing loss. Our team redesigned the iOS Phone app to support deaf and hard-of-hearing users through the full arc of a call — preparation, IVR navigation, live captions, type-to-speech, and post-call summaries. These tools belong natively in the same action sheet as Call Recording, not hidden in Settings.

What Brought Us Here

We kept returning to one fundamental question: what happens when the most basic app on your phone — the Phone app — doesn't work for you? Phones were designed around voice. Keyboards were designed around sight. We wanted to design for the people who fall through both gaps.

Apple has made progress, including Live Captions introduced in iOS 16 and expanded in iOS 26. But the people who need phone accessibility most often don't know it exists. Our project proposes making accessibility a first-class citizen of the call UI, not an afterthought in a settings menu.

User Research

I went in with assumptions. I assumed deaf users wanted technology that helps them function more like hearing people — that the goal was to close a gap. Our interviews entirely changed that framing.

One participant, Selena, is a CODA (Child of Deaf Adults) and co-president of UCSD's ASL club. She corrected my assumption that Deaf people are eager to assimilate into hearing culture. She also shared something that shaped our design significantly: "Even through an interpreter feels better than just typing to each other." ASL is a visual-spatial language with grammar, emotion, and nuance that English text can't capture. Captions are functional — but not preferred. This pushed us to include sign-to-speech (using the front camera to recognize ASL) as a future-state feature.

Before & After: Key Redesign Decisions

Change 1 — Unified setup. User testing showed separate setup steps for captions, translation, and language selection felt overwhelming. We consolidated Live Caption, Translation, and Text-to-Speech into a single "Communication Assist" sheet.

setup consolidation
in-call persistence

Change 2 — Persistent in-call controls. Users wanted accessibility tools to remain available throughout the call and requested clearer feedback when communication support features were active. We added a persistent "Communication Assist ON" pill and toggles for Live Captions and Translations directly in the call UI.

Change 3 — Caller-side transparency. The other party now sees a small notice that communication assist features are being used — building trust and reducing confusion on both ends of the call.

Process

Milestones: user interviews (P1–P3), user flows, low-fi UI sketches, Figma prototypes, Milestone 5 user feedback, iterated Figma prototypes (Milestone 5 → 7).
